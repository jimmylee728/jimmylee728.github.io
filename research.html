Technology Adoption and Schooling as Complements: The Promise of School-Based Agricultural Extension in Africa [paper coming soon]
Abstract
In sub-Saharan Africa, wide heterogeneity across farms makes other farmers’ experience a noisy guide to what suits one’s own farm, slowing technology diffusion, while low perceived returns to schooling drive students to drop out. We develop a model of why school-based agricultural extension (SBAE), which combines inquiry-based science learning, school demonstration plots, student home projects, and leadership training, addresses this dual problem. Our model builds on the target-input framework, in which a household learns a technology’s suitability only by using it in production or by observing others who do. We add a third channel: the more of a child’s time the household devotes to schooling, the more precisely it learns from the school’s production signals. Schooling and technology adoption become complements. First, schooling carries an option value on the farm: by raising the probability of future adoption, it makes attendance worthwhile even where labor-market returns are low. Second, SBAE effectively subsidizes information acquisition by delivering information through schooling, whose cost households already bear for its own returns, countering the under-supply of information in the presence of learning spillovers. We provide experimental evidence from Liberia that SBAE cost-effectively raises both agricultural technology adoption and school attendance, and use extensions of the model to study how SBAE’s design can be strengthened, including (i) how effectively inquiry-based learning raises the marginal informational return to schooling, and (ii) how effectively SBAE’s home visits aggregate households’ dispersed knowledge and feed it back to them.

Miscategorization as an Information Friction? Activating School-Based Agricultural Extension in Liberia with Parental Engagement [paper coming soon]
Abstract
Schools in developing countries often have weak ties with parents. This paper examines the extent to which programs with innovative elements—which parents may misperceive—hinge on strengthening these ties to succeed. In a three-year Liberian experiment across 197 schools, I evaluate school-based agricultural extension (SBAE) against control, cross-randomizing whether to inform parents about SBAE’s distinctive, inquiry-based learning features via promotional videos and field days. SBAE improves targeted outcomes only when paired with parental engagement: students’ households adopt 26–53% more agricultural techniques, dropouts fall 4–5 percentage points per year, absenteeism falls 28%, and studying hours rise 22%. The program is among the most cost-effective extension programs in randomized trials in sub-Saharan Africa, and over twice as cost-effective as a widely cited cash-transfer benchmark for the region (Baird et al. 2011). SBAE has strong positive training effects on students that are unaltered by parental engagement. However, without parental engagement, SBAE’s diffusion mechanisms via school demonstration farms, home projects, and intra-household communication stay muted—technology adoption effects vanish, absenteeism rises 31%, studying hours fall 20%, and parents disconnect—reducing school farm visits 12% and parent–teacher meetings 39%. Parents’ persistent avoidance of information about a new program, plus sharply reversed responses to SBAE under engagement, pose a puzzle. I discuss how standard Bayesian updating under a fixed prior, selective attention, and associative memory each fall short as standalone explanations. I propose an integrative explanation: parental engagement shifts parents’ categorization of SBAE away from schemas such as “school gardens as drudgery.”

Endogenous Model Uncertainty and Curiosity under Cognitive Limits [paper coming soon]
Abstract
People actively assign meaning to what they see and predict from it, and whether they look for more information depends on the mental model they use. I model contextual shifts in “mindsets” as a choice of how much model uncertainty to entertain: from a default mental model and a menu of latent, imprecise, and potentially more promising alternatives, an agent chooses how much attention to pay to each and how much information to acquire. This choice is disciplined by a microfounded evidence constraint that bounds motivated beliefs: the weight assigned to an alternative is capped by its likelihood relative to the default and to the other alternatives. Curiosity then arises endogenously as a drive to resolve the model uncertainty that evidence for a more promising alternative creates. The model reproduces regularities of neural remapping and unifies classic belief-updating distortions with information-acquisition anomalies. Two principles follow for how curiosity can be stimulated. First, contrast matters because an alternative is activated only when the evidence makes the default indefensible against it, and thresholds govern how attention shifts: gradually, abruptly, or not at all. Second, concentration of evidence matters because several alternatives may each beat the default and yet none be activated, however strong the evidence. I apply the framework to the design of information interventions for a school-based agricultural extension program in Liberia, and to pedagogy and learning among teachers, students, and farmers.

Communicating Parents' Beliefs to Students as a Pivotal Component of Parental Engagement: Evidence from School-Based Agricultural Extension in Liberia [paper coming soon]
Abstract
When school programs require students to introduce improved techniques on household farms, is informing students and parents about the program sufficient, or is there a case for further signaling parents’ support to students? I study this question within a randomized evaluation of school-based agricultural extension (SBAE) in Liberia, where students take school farm training into a home farming project. After parents view a promotional video of the program and students are encouraged to diffuse techniques to their parents, I randomize a nudge revealing to students their parents' optimism about their farm-management skills. Two years later, SBAE improves students' livelihoods only with the nudge: income rises 63%, on-time school fee payment 43–47%, and cost-effectiveness for attendance doubles. Analysis of mechanisms highlights a management aspect of agricultural technology adoption that can be addressed with nudges: even where parents become attentive to SBAE and adopt promoted techniques, third-party assurance is pivotal in shifting students away from assisting in subsistence farming.
