Miscategorization as an Information Friction? Activating School-Based Agricultural Education in Liberia with Parental Engagement [pdf]
Abstract
Many developing countries have under-staffed agricultural extension systems, while rural households trade off children’s schooling with farm labor. This paper shows schools can become cost-effective hubs tackling these dual challenges, but only if parent-school linkages are strengthened. In a three-year Liberian experiment across 197 schools, I evaluate school-based agricultural education (SBAE)—a voluntary program training science teachers to integrate inquiry-based learning with student-led school demonstration farms and home farming projects—estimating intent-to-treat effects on randomly sampled students. I cross-randomize whether to inform parents about this integrated program via promotional videos and field days. SBAE improves targeted outcomes when paired with parental engagement: students’ households adopt 26–53% more yield-improving farming techniques, dropouts fall 4–5 percentage points per year, absenteeism falls 28%, and studying hours rise 22%. It is among the most cost-effective agricultural extension trials, and over twice as cost-effective for improving attendance as a conditional cash transfer benchmark. SBAE’s positive training effects on teachers and students are unaltered by parental engagement. However, without parental engagement, SBAE’s diffusion mechanisms—parents’ learning from school farms, home projects, intra-household communication—stay muted. Technology adoption effects vanish, absenteeism rises 31%, studying hours fall 20%, and parents disconnect: only a third of parents visit school farms in three years, visit frequency falls 12%, and parent–teacher meetings fall 39%. Parents’ persistent avoidance of information, plus sharply reversed responses under engagement, pose a puzzle. Bayesian updating, selective attention, and associative memory each fall short. I propose an integrative explanation: parental engagement shifts parents away from viewing agriculture and education as substitutes.

Technology Adoption and Schooling as Complements: The Promise of School-Based Agricultural Education in Africa [paper available on request]
Abstract
In sub-Saharan Africa, wide heterogeneity across farms makes other households’ experience a noisy guide to what suits a household’s own farms, slowing technology diffusion, while low perceived returns to schooling drive students to drop out. We develop a model of why school-based agricultural education (SBAE), a holistic program in which trained science teachers integrate inquiry-based learning with student-led school demonstration farms and home farming projects, addresses this dual problem. Our model builds on the target-input framework, in which a household learns a technology’s suitability only by using it in production or by observing others who do. We add a third channel: the more of a child’s time the household devotes to schooling, the more precisely it learns from the school’s production signals. Schooling and technology adoption become complements. First, schooling carries an option value on the farm: by raising the probability of future adoption, it makes attendance worthwhile even where labor-market returns are low. Second, SBAE effectively subsidizes information acquisition by delivering information through schooling, whose cost households already bear for its educational returns, countering the under-supply of information in the presence of learning spillovers. We provide experimental evidence from Liberia that SBAE cost-effectively raises both agricultural technology adoption and school attendance, and use extensions of the model to study how SBAE’s design can be strengthened, including (i) how effectively inquiry-based learning raises the marginal informational return to schooling, and (ii) how effectively SBAE’s home visits aggregate households’ dispersed knowledge and feed it back to them.

Endogenous Model Uncertainty and Curiosity under Cognitive Limits [paper available on request]
Abstract
What is curiosity: what leads a person to seek the information required to explore new perspectives? Neuroscience suggests that people automatically classify a situation before choosing how to act, drawing a familiar interpretation from similar past situations (e.g., a farmer attributing a neighbor’s unusually high yield to good weather). As observations become harder to reconcile with that interpretation, people entertain less familiar alternatives. This paper treats contextual shifts in “mindsets” as a choice of how much model uncertainty to entertain. Each mental model pairs a prior with a view of how observable features combine to reveal the state, so weighting models changes how a person interprets a situation. After an initial signal, an agent with a costless default and latent alternatives chooses these weights and how much information to acquire. The key discipline is a neurally grounded evidence constraint that bounds motivated beliefs: an alternative’s weight is capped by its Bayesian posterior. Curiosity arises endogenously as a drive to resolve the resulting model uncertainty that imposes hedging costs. By letting agents seek information and move beyond the interpretation supplied by associative memory, the model revises two hallmark effects of memory-based behavioral models. First, context effects: cues that are payoff-irrelevant under the default sway decisions only when they activate an alternative under which they matter, and the curiosity this creates drives self-correction. Persistent context effects thus reflect the information environment. Second, interference effects: rather than changing which model comes to mind, rival explanations dilute one another’s support, leaving none activated and search dormant. I illustrate design principles for stimulating curiosity about new programs, pedagogies, and farming techniques using examples from development economics.

Can Communicating Parents' Beliefs to Students Be a Pivotal Component of Parental Engagement? Evidence from School-Based Agricultural Education in Liberia [paper available on request]
Abstract
When school programs require students to introduce improved techniques on household farms, is informing students and parents about the program sufficient, or is there a case for further signaling parents’ support to students? I study this question within a randomized evaluation of school-based agricultural education (SBAE) in Liberia, where students take school farm training into a home farming project. After parents view a promotional video of the program and students are encouraged to diffuse techniques to their parents, I randomize a nudge revealing to students their parents' optimism about their farm-management skills. Two years later, SBAE improves students' livelihoods only with the nudge: income rises 63%, on-time school fee payment 43–47%, and cost-effectiveness for attendance doubles. Analysis of mechanisms highlights a management aspect of agricultural technology adoption that can be addressed with nudges: even where parents become attentive to SBAE and adopt promoted techniques, third-party assurance can be pivotal in shifting students away from assisting in subsistence farming.
